# Grid world: baseline results

Generated by `go run ./cmd/judge` in 58 s. 20000 steps per run, online (agents learn as they go, no separate training), seeds [1 2 3 4 5], view 5x5. Energy is the environment's true total unease after each step; lower is better. Every number is a mean over seeds unless marked.

Agents: **idle** never acts; **random** picks uniformly among allowed actions; **reactive** is hand-written, moves towards the hottest felt cell and tries actions there, no learning; **qlearn** is tabular Q(λ) over the local view with reward = drop in felt energy on the same cells (see README); **qlearn-felt** is the identical learner with the literal reward "drop in total felt energy"; **oracle** sees the whole map and follows a scripted policy, a floor to read the others against, not a baseline.

Feel: **local** = per-cell energy of the 5x5 view; **global** = one number, the true total.

**Reading guide.** The overview below is the short answer. In one line: *maze* and *litter* separate learning from non-learning most clearly (tabular Q-learning ends at 0.12 and 0.34 of idle; the best non-learner at 0.88 and 0.89); *restore* separates them only under global feel (local feel makes it easy enough for a hand-written agent to solve); *fire* and *wells* are won by hand-written agents, and *wells* in particular separates agents with memory from agents without (the mapper, a reactive agent plus an internal map, ends at 0.10; memoryless Q-learning at 0.80). The literal reward "drop in felt energy" fails under local feel in every environment: the learner is paid for walking away from trouble, so it learns to walk away (see qlearn-felt and the look-away column).

## restore

plinths fade; write a mark to restore them. Actions: stay, north, east, south, west, mark, clear. Settled when total energy ≤ 0.

**What makes it uneasy:** a plinth whose mark has faded (shown as a faded symbol); its unease grows from 1 to 3 the longer it stays faded. Every other cell is locked. **Honest difficulty:** too easy under local feel: the hand-written reactive agent nearly solves it (0.06 of idle) because the heat is visible and the fix is one action. Kept as the first rung. Under global feel it is informative: the reactive agent has no gradient (0.77) while Q-learning learns to find and fix plinths (0.22). Note the *created by agent* column: Q-learning also learns to wipe settled plinths and then restore them (about 140 wipes per run under global feel). The closed-world accounting shows this as energy created by the agent, not resolved: a reward-shaped loop the accounting is there to expose. With learning turned off (and so exploration off) its energy rises to near idle (about 9.7): the purely greedy policy gets stuck.

## fire

**What makes it uneasy:** burning cells (1 each) and ash (0.2 each, regrows after 300 steps). Grass ignites now and then, mostly at three hotspots, and fire spreads. Doing nothing is costly: idle sits at about 13. **Honest difficulty:** fine for non-learners, hard for tabular learning. Reactive agents put fires out where they feel them (0.44); the mapper also revisits remembered hot places (0.28). Q-learning improves slowly (resolution time 401 to 305 steps; transfer ratio 0.83, so what it learns is not layout-specific) but stays far behind the hand-written agents within 20,000 steps.

## litter

litter appears; take it and put it in a bin (dropping it elsewhere only moves it). Actions: stay, north, east, south, west, take, put. Settled when total energy ≤ 0.

**What makes it uneasy:** each piece of litter (1), wherever it is, including in the agent's hands. Only putting it in a bin resolves it; putting it down elsewhere moves it. **This is the closed-world test.** Under local feel, the reactive and mapper agents resolve nothing at all, yet feel thousands of units of relief: almost all of it *look-away relief*, litter leaving the 5x5 view. Random and global-feel agents *displace* about 3,000 units per run (pick up, drop elsewhere) while resolving 16 to 22. Q-learning with the same-cells reward learns to carry litter to bins (0.34 local, 0.50 global) but still displaces about 2,300 units on the way. ## maze

**What makes it uneasy:** the unmarked cells on the route from entrance to exit (the gap), felt at the two tips of the marked trail, plus 1 per stray mark off the route. Once joined, the trail erodes now and then. *Choice:* the maze is settled by a path of marks, not by the agent reaching the exit. A path is structure written into the environment that persists after the agent leaves, can be eroded, and must be maintained; reaching the exit is a one-off event with no disturbance afterwards. **Honest difficulty:** the strongest separation. Random and reactive agents never join the trail; the reactive and mapper agents make things worse than idle (1.21, 1.26) by marking wherever they feel heat, which strews stray marks (in this environment *displaced* mostly reflects the tips moving as the trail grows or shrinks, not dumping). Q-learning joins it in most runs and maintains it (0.12 local, settled first at a median of step 7,612). But what it learned does not transfer: in a new maze the experienced agent does slightly worse than a fresh one (ratio 1.11), because exploration has decayed in states it believes it knows.

## wells

springs far apart silt up; clear them before they get bad; springs sometimes move. Actions: stay, north, east, south, west, mark, clear. Settled when total energy ≤ 1.

**What makes it uneasy:** four springs far apart, out of each other's sight; each silts up steadily (unease up to 3) until cleared. About once every 3,000 steps a spring dries up and a new one wells up elsewhere. Marks are allowed and cost nothing. **Honest difficulty:** a memory test. Agents that only react to what they feel find one spring and stay near it (reactive 0.33). The mapper, the same reactive agent plus an internal map of where it has felt heat, makes rounds and reaches 0.10; replacing its mind with a fresh one costs it (1.66 to 2.55 over the next 5,000 steps). Memoryless Q-learning barely improves (0.80). **External memory:** no baseline uses marks as memory. Random and Q-learning agents write marks everywhere because they are free (so an old mark is almost always in view, which means nothing), and erasing them changes nothing; the reactive and mapper agents write marks only as a side effect of trying every action where they feel heat, never as signposts. This test is in place for automata that might.
